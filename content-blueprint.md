# 1. Welcome and Overview

## More Playtime

### A Family Experience Guide

Toy shows bring people together through collecting, trading, storytelling, and play. Although these events are often organized around adult collectors, children are also part of the toy community. They may arrive with their families, recognize characters they love, and naturally want to look closely at the objects around them. At the same time, many items at a toy show are valuable, fragile, rare, or intended only for display. A successful family experience must respect both realities.

The More Playtime guide proposes a practical approach to welcoming children at the fictional Trinkets, Toys, and Collectibles Show in the Waterloo region. Instead of treating young visitors as a problem to control, the guide gives them constructive ways to participate. A free creative workshop offers an appropriate place to touch, make, and share. Clear signs teach children how to explore vendor tables respectfully. Vendor guidelines explain how exhibitors can protect valuable items while communicating with families in a calm and welcoming way.

This guide is designed for four connected audiences: children, parents and caregivers, vendors, and event organizers. Each audience receives information in a form suited to its needs. Organizers receive a detailed proposal that can be evaluated and implemented. Vendors receive direct recommendations for arranging tables and responding to young visitors. Children receive short, positive rules with a strong visual hierarchy. Families receive a catalogue of proposed activities they can understand at a glance.

The event described in this portfolio is fictional. Workshop schedules, activity locations, staffing plans, and catalogue entries are proposals rather than confirmed event details. External information used to support safety, accessibility, and communication recommendations will be documented in the References section.

# 2. Genre One: Build-a-Toy Workshop Proposal

**Document type:** Program proposal  
**Prepared for:** Event organizers  
**Proposed program:** Build-a-Toy Workshop  
**Proposed location:** Family Activity Zone  
**Recommended age range:** 6–12; younger children may participate with a caregiver  
**Proposed session length:** 25–30 minutes

## Executive Summary

This proposal recommends adding a free Build-a-Toy Workshop to the Trinkets, Toys, and Collectibles Show. The workshop would give children a clearly identified place to design a paper toy character using simple craft materials. It would support the show's community-building goals while also addressing a practical challenge: children need an appropriate way to interact with toys in a space where many objects cannot be handled.

The workshop is intended as a structured drop-in activity, not a childcare service. Parents and caregivers would remain responsible for the children in their care. Two volunteers would facilitate each scheduled session, explain the activity, monitor materials, answer questions, and reset the tables between groups. Final staffing levels and room capacity would need to be confirmed after a venue is selected.

## Purpose and Rationale

The proposed workshop has three purposes. First, it gives children meaningful access to the event. A child who is repeatedly told not to touch anything may understand the restriction but still feel that the show was not designed for them. A creative activity changes the message from “do not participate” to “participate here.”

Second, the workshop supports vendors and collectors. By providing a designated hands-on experience, organizers can direct families toward an area where touching and experimentation are expected. This does not eliminate the need for supervision or careful displays, but it can reduce frustration at vendor tables and make behavioural expectations easier to explain.

Third, the workshop contributes to the show's goal of becoming a community “third space.” Children can create a character, talk about favourite toys, and see collecting as a form of storytelling rather than only buying and selling. Families who do not plan to make expensive purchases can still take part in the event in a memorable way.

## Target Participants

The primary audience is children ages 6–12. The activity uses drawing, colouring, folding, and simple assembly, so children will have different levels of independence. Younger children may participate when assisted by a parent or caregiver. Instructions should be available in both written and demonstrated form so that participants are not required to read a long set of directions before beginning.

The workshop must not be advertised as supervised childcare. Children should remain with, or within the direct supervision of, their parent or caregiver. The final policy should define the age at which a caregiver must sit beside a child and should be reviewed against reliable local guidance before publication. **[SOURCE/POLICY REVIEW REQUIRED]**

## Proposed Activity

Each participant will design a paper toy character. A volunteer will provide a cardstock template showing a simple figure with large spaces for drawing. Children can give the character a name, choose its special feature, decorate it, and complete a short “collector card” containing three prompts:

1. My toy's name is…
2. My toy is special because…
3. My toy belongs in a collection about…

Children may take their work home or, with caregiver permission, place a copy on the Community Toy Wall. The wall would display paper creations rather than personal photographs, reducing the need to collect children's identifying information. Organizers should not publish a child's name, image, or work online without a separate and clearly explained permission process. **[PRIVACY SOURCE REQUIRED]**

## Proposed Session Structure

| Time | Stage | Volunteer responsibility |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–5 minutes | Welcome and demonstration | Explain the project and show a completed example |
| 5–20 minutes | Design time | Support participants and monitor shared materials |
| 20–25 minutes | Optional sharing | Invite children to describe their characters without requiring them to speak |
| 25–30 minutes | Take-home and reset | Return work, clean surfaces, and prepare for the next group |

Four sessions could be scheduled during the show, with breaks between them for cleaning, volunteer rotation, and general family use of the area. A possible schedule is 10:00 a.m., 11:30 a.m., 1:30 p.m., and 3:00 p.m. These times are proposals and should not appear in public-facing materials until approved by the organizers.

## Space and Staffing Requirements

The Family Activity Zone should be visible and easy to find without blocking vendor traffic. It should include clear entry and exit paths, enough room for caregivers to remain nearby, chairs that can be moved as needed, and a quiet edge where a child can pause without leaving the caregiver's supervision. The final layout must comply with the selected venue's occupancy, accessibility, and emergency-exit requirements. **[VENUE AND ACCESSIBILITY SOURCES REQUIRED]**

At least two designated volunteers should facilitate each session. One volunteer can welcome participants and explain the activity while the other manages materials and supports families already at the tables. Volunteers should know who to contact for first aid, missing-child situations, behavioural concerns, spills, and accessibility requests. They should not photograph children, provide one-to-one childcare, or take a child to a private area.

## Expected Benefits

For children, the workshop creates an active role in an event that might otherwise consist mainly of looking and shopping. For caregivers, it provides a free, structured activity and a natural pause during a crowded event. For vendors, it gives organizers a positive alternative to offer children who want a hands-on experience. For organizers, it demonstrates that family inclusion has been planned rather than added as an afterthought.

## Evaluation

The workshop can be evaluated without collecting children's personal information. Volunteers can record the number of participants, note whether sessions reached capacity, count the number of completed templates used, and document recurring questions or material shortages. Caregivers may be invited to leave an optional anonymous comment. After the event, organizers can use this information to decide whether to repeat, expand, or revise the program.

# 3. Genre Two: Welcoming Young Visitors — Vendor Guidelines

**Document type:** Vendor communication guide  
**Primary audience:** Toy-show vendors  
**Working purpose:** Protect collectibles while creating respectful interactions with children and caregivers

## Advance Notice

Children and families will be actively welcomed at the proposed Trinkets, Toys, and Collectibles Show. Young visitors may be enthusiastic, curious, and unfamiliar with collector-show expectations. Organizers will provide children's rules, family activities, and volunteer support. Vendors can help by arranging displays clearly, communicating expectations in positive language, and asking for assistance before a situation becomes a conflict.

This guide does not require vendors to allow every item to be touched. It asks vendors to make boundaries visible and understandable. A welcoming table can still protect rare, fragile, or expensive merchandise.

## Prepare Your Display

Use the following three-level system to communicate handling expectations:

- **Green — Welcome to Touch:** Items are durable, appropriately priced, and intentionally available for closer inspection.
- **Yellow — Please Ask First:** Visitors should speak with the vendor before lifting or handling the item.
- **Red — Display Only:** The item is fragile, valuable, rare, or not intended to be handled.

Place high-value and fragile collectibles away from the front edge of the table. Consider using closed display cases, clear barriers, stable risers, or table-behind displays where appropriate. Keep boxes, bags, cords, and personal belongings out of visitor pathways. Price labels and handling signs should be large enough to read before a visitor reaches for an object.

Vendors are responsible for choosing display methods appropriate to their own merchandise. Organizers should review the final guidance against venue rules and should not claim that these measures eliminate the risk of damage or loss.

## Communicate Clearly and Respectfully

Children may not understand the difference between a toy intended for play and a collectible intended for display. Give one clear instruction at a time and explain what the child can do next.

| Avoid | Try instead |
|---|---|
| “Don't touch anything.” | “Please ask me before picking that one up.” |
| “That's too expensive for you.” | “This one is display-only, but you can look closely from here.” |
| “You should know better.” | “Some toys here have special handling rules.” |
| “Where are your parents?” | “Is your grown-up nearby? Let's get their help.” |

When speaking with a caregiver, describe the specific behaviour or concern rather than making a judgment about the child. For example, say, “This figure is fragile, so I need it to stay in the case,” rather than, “Your child is being careless.” If a conversation becomes tense, pause the transaction and ask an event volunteer or organizer to assist.

## Create a Welcoming Table

Where practical, identify one small part of the display that children can explore safely. This might include durable low-cost items, a sample object the vendor is comfortable demonstrating, or a question card inviting visitors to ask about the history of a toy. Vendors are not expected to provide gifts or discounts, but a brief explanation can help a young visitor feel included even when an item cannot be handled or purchased.

Do not assume that children are uninterested in collecting or unable to understand it. Use simple language without speaking down to them. When possible, address the child who asked the question while keeping the caregiver included in any discussion involving prices, purchases, personal information, or conflict.

## When You Need Help

Contact a volunteer or organizer when:

- a child appears to be separated from a caregiver;
- a visitor repeatedly ignores a clearly explained handling boundary;
- an item is damaged or a payment dispute begins;
- a visitor becomes threatening, discriminatory, or abusive;
- a spill, obstruction, or unsafe condition affects the aisle;
- you are unsure how to respond to an accessibility request.

Vendors should not physically restrain a child, escort a child to a private location, threaten punishment, photograph a child for identification, or post a conflict involving a child on social media. Missing-child, emergency, and incident procedures must be provided by the organizers before the event. **[POLICY AND SOURCE REVIEW REQUIRED]**

# 4. Genre Three: Kids' Rules Poster

## Welcome to the Toy Show!

### 1. Look with your eyes first

Some toys are special collectibles. Take a good look before reaching.

### 2. Ask before touching

The vendor will tell you which toys you may hold.

### 3. Walk carefully

Walking keeps you, other visitors, and the toys safe.

### 4. Stay with your grown-up

Explore the show together and tell them before going anywhere.

### 5. Use a calm indoor voice

Lots of people are sharing the same space.

### 6. Have fun and be kind

Ask questions, share space, and enjoy discovering something new.

**Poster design note:** Each rule should appear as a large card with a simple icon, a short heading, one supporting sentence, high colour contrast, and enough spacing to be read at a child's eye level. The language uses positive actions rather than a wall of prohibitions.
